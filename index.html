The problem

You already know where the hours go: cleaning, mixing, cooking, cooling, inoculating, incubating, cleaning again.

When Cornell surveyed 139 specialty growers, the top three barriers to growing the business were labor, production facilities, and growing output to meet demand. Production knowledge came last. Nearly two thirds already had more demand than they could supply.

The ceiling mostly isn't sales, and it isn't know-how. It's the hours and the square footage that go into making blocks. Cornell also costed five indoor farms: making blocks on site, labor was about 80% of what a block cost them; buying them in, about 42%.

That's the part we're building around.

What we're building

A ready-to-fruit block you buy instead of make. Prep hours go back into growing and selling. Prep space becomes fruiting space.

Right now we're finishing development testing, building the first prototypes of the machinery, working through National Science Foundation funding, and talking to growers so that what gets built is grounded in what the industry actually needs rather than in what we assume it needs.

How it works

Three things at once.

A patent-pending method that redesigns the prep process to cut cost and raise the quality of the block. That's the piece I can't say more about while the patent work is in progress, and I'd rather say so plainly than talk around it.

Custom machinery. Several steps in substrate prep have no good equipment behind them, so they get done by hand. Those machines are designed, and some of them are already prototyped.

Scale. Making blocks a thousand at a time costs less per block than making them a hundred at a time. Almost none of the industry's substrate is made at that volume today.

Where this started

In 2020 I worked out what nineteen different mushrooms would cost to grow and what each one would sell for. I was trying to answer one question: could gourmet mushrooms ever be cheap?

The answer was that the mushroom is never the expensive part. The block is.

About five years of hands-on testing since then. I restarted the mycology club at Cal Poly and ran it for two years, built a flow hood, a fruiting chamber, a small lab and a greenhouse, and ran roughly thirty of my own trials on getting substrate colonized without the usual prep. A good number of them didn't work.

One piece of it: aerated liquid culture of blue oyster in a bioreactor — growth parameters, how the culture answers different nutrients, keeping flocculation under control. Twelve runs over about eighteen months, six of which came off.

Before this, my work was contamination control in aerospace, on hypergolic propulsion systems, where something that isn't clean enough doesn't fail politely. Keeping a process clean at volume is the same problem here, at a different scale and at a price a farm can pay.

There's a written technical plan behind all of it: about 470 pages drawing on more than 500 sources, and a bench campaign running now. Patent pending — provisional filed July 2026. Rocky Mountain Mushroom Farm LLC is a Colorado company, formed 2025.

Questions

What will it cost?

I won't quote a price I can't stand behind yet. The target is to beat what a block costs you to make once your own labor is counted.

When can I get one?

Not this year. Bench work finishes first, then trials. The survey is how you get into the first round of those.

What species?

Blue oyster first, then lion's mane and shiitake — the sawdust-block species. Which one moves fastest after oyster depends partly on what growers tell us.

Buying blocks means paying up front and waiting to get paid.

It does, and Cornell put that gap at eight to twelve weeks. It's the strongest argument against buying blocks at all, and any terms we offer have to answer it. There's a question about it on the survey, and how growers answer it will shape what we do about it.
