The problem

You already know where the hours go: cleaning, mixing, cooking, cooling, inoculating, incubating, cleaning again.

When Cornell surveyed 139 specialty growers, the top three barriers to growing the business were labor, production facilities, and growing output to meet demand1 — so the ceiling isn't sales, and it's not know-how. It's the hours and the square footage that go into making blocks. Cornell costed five indoor farms and found the same thing in the accounts: on the farms making their own blocks, 80% of what they spent went to labor and 20% to materials, while on the farms buying blocks ready to fruit labor dropped to 42%.2 That's the part I'm aiming to build around.

What I'm building

A ready-to-fruit block you buy instead of make. Hours go back into growing and selling, and more square footage can go towards fruiting.

A brewery doesn't malt its own barley, a bakery doesn't mill its own flour. Farmers buy one-year-old asparagus crowns, they don't sow them from seed. Fruiting, packing and selling will always be local problems — and they will always rely on local mushroom farms — but the target is to cost less than making your own, including materials and labor. Right now I'm finishing development testing, building the first prototypes of the machinery, and working through National Science Foundation funding.

How it works

Three things, and they only pay off together: a method that changes what a block costs to make, machinery that exists because nothing off the shelf does the job, and enough scale for either of those to matter.

A patent-pending method that redesigns how a block gets made. I got into the microbiology side of this early and never really got back out - specifically mycelial enzymatic expression, which is a fancy way of saying the fungus already does a lot of the work itself if you set things up right, so a farm ends up paying in labor and process time for what the biology would do on its own. I spent about five years chasing that, and somewhere in there it turned into a plan that runs 470 pages with more than 500 sources behind it. The method that came out of it is patent pending, filed July 2026. I'm finishing up development tests now to pin down what the yield and the cost actually are, because I'd rather hand you a real number than a hopeful one.

Custom machinery. Several steps in substrate prep have no good equipment behind them, so they get done by hand. My work before this was contamination control in aerospace, on hypergolic propulsion systems, where a process that isn't clean enough doesn't fail politely. I'm using that background to design machinery that is cheap to build, keeps contamination out, and scales. Those machines are designed, and some of them are already prototyped.

Scale. Making blocks a thousand at a time costs less per block than making them a hundred at a time. Most farms that make their own are working a batch at a time, on equipment sized for a batch.2

Questions

When can I get one?

Not this year. Bench work finishes first, then trials. The survey is how you get into the first round of those.

What species?

Blue oyster first, then lion's mane and shiitake — the sawdust-block species. Which one moves fastest after oyster depends partly on what growers tell me.

Isn't buying blocks just what you do before you scale up?

That's the honest objection, and it's the one I take most seriously — most people who buy blocks are buying them on the way to making their own. Buying an input stops being a beginner move at the point where the person making it is doing something you can't do as cheaply in your own building. That's the whole bet, and it's a fair thing to hold me to.
